What to do when the throat goes scratchy at 9 pm

The first hour of a cold is when the marketing is loudest and the evidence is quietest. So here is what to do at the first sign of a cold: eight cards, in the order the evening happens.

0 of 8 done
  1. 1Tonight

    Protect seven hours of sleep. Tonight, not this week.

    In the Pittsburgh exposure study, 164 adults wore a sleep sensor for a week and were then given cold virus by nose drops in a hotel. People who had been sleeping under six hours a night were 4.2 times as likely to develop a cold as people sleeping over seven. The bedtime alarm is the cheapest thing on this list.

    One controlled exposure study, sleep measured not self-reported

    Source: Prather et al., SLEEP, September 2015 (summary)

  2. 2Right now

    Wash hands and stop sharing the mug.

    Not glamorous. The CDC’s handwashing fact sheet puts the effect of handwashing education at 16 to 21% fewer respiratory illnesses, colds included, in the general population. Your own cold is already brewing; this is for the rest of the house.

    Public-health estimate across many studies

    Source: CDC, Handwashing Facts

  3. 3At the pharmacy

    Vitamin C: scratch the tube before you buy it.

    What the pooled trials say

    29 comparisons, 11,306 people: regular vitamin C did not reduce how often people caught colds (risk ratio 0.97).

    Taken every day, all season, colds were about 8% shorter in adults and 14% shorter in children.

    Started after symptoms began: no consistent effect on length or severity (7 comparisons, 3,249 colds).

    So the tube you buy tonight is unlikely to change this cold. The exception in the review: people under heavy physical stress, such as marathon runners, where regular use roughly halved the risk.

    Scratch the claim with a finger or the mouse.

    Source: Hemilä & Chalker, Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews 2013, CD000980 (summary)

  4. 4At the pharmacy

    Zinc lozenges: scratch the box.

    What the pooled trials say

    34 studies, 8,526 people. Taking zinc to prevent colds: little or no difference in how many colds people caught.

    Taking zinc once a cold starts: colds may be about two days shorter, but the review rates that evidence low certainty.

    Side effects were more common with zinc: taste changes, nausea, stomach upset. The review says for some people they may outweigh the benefit.

    A maybe, with a price. Decide with your pharmacist, not the box.

    Scratch the claim with a finger or the mouse.

    Source: Nault et al., Cochrane Review on zinc for the common cold, May 2024 (summary; CIDRAP report)

  5. 5At the pharmacy

    Vitamin D: scratch the winter-immunity ad.

    What the pooled trials say

    The 2025 update pooled 40 trials and 61,589 people on vitamin D versus placebo for acute respiratory infections.

    Odds ratio 0.94, confidence interval 0.88 to 1.00. The interval touches 1.00, so the authors call it no statistically significant protection.

    No subgroup changed the picture: not age, not starting vitamin D level, not dose, not daily versus weekly.

    This is about preventing colds, not about treating a deficiency a blood test found. That second thing is a conversation with your clinician.

    Scratch the claim with a finger or the mouse.

    Source: Jolliffe et al., The Lancet Diabetes & Endocrinology 2025;13:307-320 (open-access record)

  6. 6For the next few days

    Do the boring things the CDC lists for feeling better.

    Rest. Fluids. A clean humidifier or cool-mist vaporiser. Saline spray or drops. Steam from a bowl or the shower. Lozenges (not under age 4). Honey for cough, age 1 and up. Adults can use over-the-counter pain and fever relievers for temporary relief; the CDC says check with a pharmacist first. None of these shorten the cold. They make the week easier, and that counts.

    Guidance for symptom relief, not a cure

    Source: CDC, Treatment of Common Cold

  7. 7Skip

    Do not go looking for antibiotics.

    Colds are viral; more than 200 viruses cause them, rhinoviruses most often. The CDC’s line is blunt: antibiotics don’t work against viruses and won’t help you feel better, and the side effects run from rashes to C. diff infection. Save the ask for a bacterial infection a clinician actually diagnoses.

    Settled, per CDC

    Source: CDC, About Common Cold

  8. 8Watch for

    Know the five signs that mean call someone.

    A cold usually lasts under a week. The CDC says seek care for trouble breathing or fast breathing, dehydration, fever lasting longer than four days, symptoms lasting more than ten days without improving, or symptoms that improve and then come back worse. If you are at higher risk of severe COVID-19 or flu, contact a provider right away even if it seems mild, and test.

    Public-health guidance

    Source: CDC, Treatment of Common Cold

Questions people type at 9 pm

Does vitamin C prevent colds?

For the general population, no: across 29 trial comparisons and 11,306 people, regular vitamin C did not reduce how often people caught colds. Taken daily all season it shortened colds by about 8% in adults and 14% in children, and starting it once symptoms appear showed no consistent effect. Body Unpacked’s Evidence Lens covers exactly this question, split into separate statements with the study type and named source behind each.

Should I take zinc at the first sign of a cold?

The May 2024 Cochrane review (34 studies, 8,526 people) found zinc may shorten a cold by around two days, with low-certainty evidence, and more side effects such as taste changes and nausea. For prevention it made little or no difference. The review suggests the side effects may outweigh the benefit for some people; ask a pharmacist.

Does vitamin D stop you catching colds?

The 2025 Lancet Diabetes & Endocrinology meta-analysis of 40 trials and 61,589 people found an odds ratio of 0.94 with a confidence interval of 0.88 to 1.00, which the authors describe as no statistically significant protection. That is about prevention in general; a measured deficiency is a separate question for your clinician.

How long does a cold last, and when should I see a doctor?

The CDC says colds usually last less than a week, and adults average two to three a year. Seek care for trouble breathing, dehydration, fever over four days, symptoms over ten days without improving, or symptoms that get better then worse. Higher-risk people should contact a provider early and test for COVID-19 or flu.

What actually helps me feel better?

The CDC list: rest, fluids, a clean humidifier, saline spray, steam, lozenges (age 4+), honey for cough (age 1+), and over-the-counter pain or fever relievers for adults after checking with a pharmacist. Antibiotics do not help a cold.
