ALL CASES ARE FICTIONAL

“50% lower” sounds enormous.

But 2 out of 100 compared with 1 out of 100 is a difference of one percentage point.

The numbers, time period and study design belong beside the claim.

01 · Association

The garden-club survey

A fictional survey asks 300 adults whether they belong to a garden club and how rested they feel. Club members report feeling more rested. Nobody is assigned to a club, and the survey does not measure work schedules or age.

Which headline stays within this evidence?

Read the answer and explanation

Best-supported headline: Garden-club membership is linked with reported rest in this survey.

The survey finds an association. Other differences between the groups could explain it. The result does not establish that joining a club caused the difference, or that no relationship exists.

Ask next: What other differences between the groups were measured?

02 · Study population

A finding in a dish

In a fictional laboratory experiment, compound Q changes a measured signal in cells grown in a dish. There are no animal or human results and no safety data.

What is an accurate headline?

Read the answer and explanation

Best-supported headline: Compound Q changes a signal in laboratory-grown cells.

A cell result describes what happened in that laboratory setting. It does not show benefit or safety in people. Moving from a laboratory finding to a practical health claim requires additional evidence.

Ask next: Has this result been investigated in people, and with what outcomes?

03 · Comparison

The reminder-card trial

In a fictional trial, 200 adults are randomly assigned to a reminder card or no card. After one week, 70 of 100 in the card group and 50 of 100 in the comparison group complete a scheduled practice. The summary gives no uncertainty estimates or longer follow-up.

Which headline is supported?

Read the answer and explanation

Best-supported headline: Completion was 70% versus 50% after one week in this trial.

The headline reports the observed result and time period. Random assignment supports a more informative comparison, but this short summary does not show a universal effect, lasting benefit or the precision of the estimated difference.

Ask next: How precise is the estimated difference, and did the effect last?

Read the source guidance

04 · Numbers

The missing denominator

A fictional study summary reports a particular outcome in 2 of 100 people in group A and 1 of 100 in group B over the same month. The headline says the rate in B is “50% lower.”

Which addition makes the numbers clearer?

Read the answer and explanation

Best-supported headline: The observed rates are 2% and 1%: a difference of 1 percentage point.

The relative difference is (2 − 1) ÷ 2 = 50%. The absolute difference is 2% − 1% = 1 percentage point, or 1 fewer outcome per 100 people. These counts alone do not tell us whether the result is causal or statistically reliable.

Ask next: What outcome, time period, study design and uncertainty do the numbers describe?

Read the source guidance

05 · Personal story

One enthusiastic review

In a fictional review, one person starts using a coloured journal and says their evenings improve. At the same time, their work schedule changes. There is no comparison group or systematic measurement.

Which headline fits?

Read the answer and explanation

Best-supported headline: One person reports better evenings after several changes.

The experience can be meaningful to that person, but it cannot isolate the journal’s effect. Neither a universal benefit nor a definite lack of effect follows from the account.

Ask next: Is there a comparison that can separate the journal from the other changes?

Read the source guidance

06 · Scope

A test in experienced adults

A fictional study tests a new set of instructions with 40 experienced adult cyclists for one afternoon. It records whether they can identify parts on a practice bicycle. It does not include children, beginners or road riding.

Which claim matches the task and participants?

Read the answer and explanation

Best-supported headline: The instructions were tested on part identification by experienced adults.

The measured outcome is identifying parts, not riding safely. The people and setting also matter. Results for experienced adults do not automatically establish the same outcome for other groups.

Ask next: Does the tested population, setting and outcome match the claim?

Use it with a group

Give each pair one case. One person reads the evidence; the other chooses a headline and points to the detail that supports it. Swap roles for the next case. Finish by writing one question the study summary leaves unanswered.
