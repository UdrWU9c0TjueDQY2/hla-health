Cardio or strength training on Ozempic: which one?

Both, but if you only have the energy for one, make it strength. In the one randomized trial that tested a GLP-1 medication with and without a structured exercise program (Lundgren and colleagues, New England Journal of Medicine, May 2021), the people who combined liraglutide with exercise cut body-fat percentage by 3.9 points over a year, about twice the drop seen with medication alone or exercise alone. The drug does the appetite work. Exercise decides what the lost weight is made of.

The U.S. Physical Activity Guidelines (CDC) ask every adult for muscle-strengthening activity on two or more days a week on top of aerobic activity. On a weight-loss medication that is not optional extra credit; it is the part that tells your body to keep muscle. Here are the five ways we have watched that routine fall apart, and the fix for each.

Walking is the whole plan

Steps are the easiest thing to do when you feel flat, so steps become everything. Your heart benefits. Your muscles get no signal to stay.

Fix: keep the walks and add two 20-minute strength sessions. Squats to a chair, a hinge, a push, a pull, a carry. That is a complete session.

Protein disappears with your appetite

Semaglutide produced about 15% average weight loss over 68 weeks in the STEP 1 trial (Wilding et al., NEJM, March 2021), largely by making you want far less food. The meals you skip first are often the ones with the protein in them.

Fix: agree a daily protein target with your clinician, then log it by meal period so you can see which meal is the gap. Sturdy lets you log grams from a label, use quick-add foods, or switch to simple meal checkmarks if counting is too much.

All-or-nothing weeks

A 45-minute session on a good day, then nothing for nine days because the next three were queasy. The routine never gets a rhythm.

Fix: plan sessions you can do on a bad day. A five-minute movement break still counts. Missed days should show as unfilled, not as failures, so you come back without a guilt tax.

Pushing through pain or nausea

Lifting while dizzy, or forcing a sore shoulder through a push, ends with an injury and a quiet decision to stop. Injection day is a common trap.

Fix: flag sore areas before a session and have those movements swapped out; on rough days do mobility instead. Difficulty should only change when you choose. If you have dizziness, pain or a recent injury, check with your clinician before exercising.

The scale is your only progress number

If weight is the only thing you track, a plateau week looks like failure even when you got stronger. People quit on plateaus, not on bad workouts.

Fix: record something the scale cannot see: sessions completed, loads you used, a 30-second chair-stand count (Sturdy uses the CDC STEADI protocol), protein consistency. Your own history, with no age norms and no scores.
